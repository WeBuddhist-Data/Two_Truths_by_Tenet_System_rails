# The Precious Lamp

*An Excellent Explanation that Thoroughly Clarifies the Meaning of the Two Truths according to the System of the Four Tenets*

Herein lies “The Precious Lamp: An Elegant Explanation that Thoroughly Clarifies the Meaning of the Two Truths According to the System of the Four Tenet Systems.”

Homage to the Guru Mañjuśrī!

## 1. The Presentation of the Two Truths

Here, the presentation of the two truths is explained in three parts: the purpose of teaching the two truths, the explanation of the two truths according to the four tenet systems, and the concise presentation of the differing assertions of the higher and lower tenet systems.

### 1.1 The Purpose of Teaching the Two Truths

The first, the purpose of teaching the two truths, is as follows: The Teacher, the Lord of Sages, taught dependent origination as the method for those to be trained to attain high status and definite goodness. By firmly grasping its meaning, one develops conviction in the unerring nature of karma and its results, which are the causes of favourable rebirths, and one gives rise to the realization of the selflessness of persons and phenomena. If the realization of selflessness is generated, one will turn away from cyclic existence, because these afflictions, such as attachment, have the view of self as their root. Since such selflessness is seen through realizing the meaning of dependent origination, the sutras state: “Whoever sees dependent origination sees the dharma.”

If one asks: The sutras state*,*

“Whatever is born from conditions is unborn; it has no self-nature (*\*svabhāva, rang bzhin*) of arising.”

Since it is stated that things that are dependent originations do not arise by means of their self-nature (\*svabhāva, rang bzhin), how can one posit that they are arisen? For if something is born from a seed, it is arisen, and thus it is contradictory for it to be unborn. If a single thing were to be both arisen and unarisen, it would lack the defining characteristic of mutually exclusive existence, and thus all conventional presentations would be destroyed.

This objection arises due to not knowing the distinction between the two truths; therefore, it is important to know the distinction between the two truths. The fact that the Buddha sometimes stated that things arise because they are dependent originations, and at other times stated that they are unborn because they are dependent originations, is taught in reliance upon the two truths. As the *Mūlamadhyamakakārikā* (Chapter 24) states:

“The Buddhas’ teaching of the Dharma
Is perfectly based upon two truths.”

As the *Prasannapadā* states:

“This is because the teaching of the Dharma by the Buddha Bhagavāns proceeds in reliance upon the two truths.”

Therefore, when considering the conventional truth, things that are dependent originations arise; and when considering the ultimate truth, things that are dependent originations are without arising. Thus, the two—arising and non-arising—are established by considering them in relation to their respective truths.

If one does not know the presentation of the two truths, one is confused regarding the Sage’s word. By thinking that because things are taught to be without arising in the ultimate sense, they are also without arising conventionally, or by thinking that because dependent origination is taught conventionally, there is arising, cessation, and so forth in the ultimate sense, one does not transcend the state of this wrong view and fails to comprehend the meaning of reality (*\*tattva, de kho na nyid*). As the Mūlamadhyamakakārikā (Chapter 24) states:

“Those who do not understand
The distinction between these two truths
Do not understand the profound
reality (*tattva, de kho na nyid*) of the Buddha’s teaching.”

### 1.2 The Two Truths According to the Four Tenet Systems

The second part, the explanation of the two truths according to the four tenet systems, has four sections: the explanation of the Vaibhāṣika system, the Sautrāntika system, the Cittamātra system, and the Mādhyamika system.

#### 1.2.1 The Vaibhāṣika System

The first, the Vaibhāṣika system, is as stated in the Abhidharmakośa (Chapter 6):

“That which, when broken, or [when] other [parts] are excluded by the mind,
the cognition of it no longer occurs:
like a pot or water, exists conventionally.
The ultimately existent is otherwise.”

The Auto-commentary states: “That which, when broken into parts, no longer allows for its own cognition, exists conventionally; for example, a pot. When it is broken into shards, the cognition of the pot does not occur. That which, when other phenomena are mentally excluded, no longer evokes its own cognition, should also be understood as existing conventionally; for example, water. When the mind excludes phenomena such as form and so forth, the cognition of water does not occur. But since conventionally the name [‘pot’, ‘water’] is applied to those very [phenomena], those who say, by force of convention, “there is a pot, there is water,” speak the truth and not a falsehood. Thus it is the conventional truth.”

In this context, “conventional” refers to that which is mutually dependent. When shapes such as a bulbous form, and substances such as the form, odour, and taste of water are mutually dependent, it is a truthful statement—and not a falsehood—to say that a pot or water exists on that basis; thus, it is called a “conventional truth.” “Conventional truth,” “established conventionally,” “existing conventionally,” and “designated existent” are synonymous.

The former of the two examples provided illustrates the conventionality of shape, while the latter illustrates the conventionality of collection. Although the conventionality of collection pervades the conventionality of shape, the conventionality of shape does not pervade the conventionality of collection. Because things like a pot exist in dependence upon other things, such as shards, it is a “conventionality dependent on other conventionalities,” and it is possible for it to be destroyed by breaking as well as by mental exclusion. Because water exists in dependence upon form, it is a “conventionality dependent on other substances,” and it is possible for it to be destroyed only by mental exclusion, not by physical breaking.

Other than that is the ultimate truth, for even when it is broken or other dharmas are mentally excluded, the cognition of it still continues. For example, when considering form, whether it is broken down into subtle particles or whether properties such as taste are mentally excluded, the cognition of the nature of form itself still continues; the same should be viewed regarding feelings and so forth. Because it exists in the ultimate sense, it is called an ultimate truth.

Here, “ultimate” refers to that which does not rely on other dharmas to be identified, but is established independently. Ultimate truth, ultimately established, ultimately existent, and substantially existent all mean the same thing.

According to the Vaibhāṣikas, because form itself is an aggregate, the term “aggregate of form” is a compound [that indicates] a common locus; thus, while each of the five aggregates is individually substantially existent, their continuum or collection exists only conventionally.

In the desire realm, for a coarse form to be established, it requires a collection of eight substantial particles: the four primary elements and the four derivative elements—visible form, odour, taste, and tangibility. In the higher realms, because there is no odour or taste, a collection of six substantial particles is required. Because the cognition that apprehends these substantial particles as they are does not rely on other substances, they are ultimate truths, and they are asserted to be part-less things.

Regarding the uncompounded, there are three: space and the two types of cessation. These are ultimate truths. These three are permanent things because they are not produced by causes and conditions, and thus are uncompounded; because they exist without changing throughout the three times, they are permanent; because they are independent substances, they are things (*vastu, dngos po*) in the sense of nature. Because they are the object-condition of consciousness, they are things (*vastu, dngos po*) in the sense of object. And because they perform the function of blocking obstructive contact and afflictions, they are things (*vastu, dngos po*).

Some assert that if something is an ultimate truth, it must be part-less. Others assert that although there are things with parts that are established as ultimately existent, positing them must ultimately terminate in [something] part-less. There are three types of part-less entities: spatially part-less particles, part-less moments of cognition, and uncompounded part-less entities, which are distinct from the former two. The meaning of “part-less” is defined as that which is its own entity and is devoid of spatial parts or temporal parts that are other than itself.

#### 1.2.2 The Sautrāntika System

Second, the explanation of the Sautrāntika system has three parts: the definitions of the two truths, the etymological explanation, and the explanation of related topics.

**Regarding the first, the definitions of the two truths**, the Pramāṇavārttika (Chapter 3) states:

“Whatever is ultimately capable of performing a function
Is what is ultimately existent here.
Others are conventionally existent.
These are explained as specifically and generally characterised.”

Commentary: All those [things that] are capable of performing a function are all ultimately existent; whereas other phenomena that are unable to perform a function are conventionally existent. Therefore, a phenomenon that is ultimately capable of performing a function is the definition of ultimate truth. Ultimate truth, ultimately existent, and specifically characterised (*svalakṣaṇa, rang mtshan*) are synonymous. A phenomenon that is unable to ultimately perform a function is the definition of conventional truth. Conventional truth, conventionally existent, and generally characterised (*sāmānyalakṣaṇa, spyi mtshan*) are synonymous.

The Pramāṇavārttika (Chapter 3) states:

“Because of being able and unable to perform functions,”

and,

“Because of being similar and dissimilar,
and because of being and not being the objects of words;
and because, when other causes are present,
a cognition arises and does not.”

Commentary: Specifically characterised (*svalakṣaṇa, rang mtshan*) possesses four distinctive features: the feature of its essence, which is the capacity to perform a function by producing its own result; the feature of how it appears, which is being “dissimilar” in that it appears uniquely to the cognition for which it is the appearing object; the feature of the object-possessor, where it is not an object of words because its essence cannot fully appear to a cognition merely through the expressive words as the governing condition; and the feature of action, which is that even when there are other causes besides the object itself, such as verbal conventions and attention, if it were not established through the object’s own mode of existence, the cognition that realizes it cannot arise.

The opposite of these four are the features of the generally characterised (*sāmānyalakṣaṇa, spyi mtshan*). There is a meaning to “appearing uniquely to the cognition for which it is the appearing object.” Although a pot, for instance, is a generality and thus follows upon many of its own instances, for the cognition for which it is the appearing object, the commonality that follows upon its own instances does not appear; rather, there arises a unique feature in which place, time, and nature are unmixed. Therefore, because it is a dharma that must be realized by the cognition for which it is the appearing object through the arising of its own unique sign, it is Specifically characterised (*svalakṣaṇa, rang mtshan*). For that reason, it cannot appear in its complete essence to a cognition arising from words, and thus it is not an object of words; furthermore, the cognition that realizes it does not arise merely through verbal conventions and mental attention.

Generally characterised phenomena are the reverse of that. Because they must be realized by bringing their general aspect to mind, without there being any possibility of their unique sign appearing to the cognition for which they are the appearing object, they are generally characterised (*sāmānyalakṣaṇa, spyi mtshan*).  For that reason, because their essence appears completely to a cognition arising from words, they are objects of words; and because they appear to the cognition merely through the presence of other causes such as verbal conventions and mental attention, they are explained as being merely designated by the conceptual cognition (\**vikalpa, rtog pa*).

Regarding the meaning of the statement, “These are explained as specifically and generally characterised,” some explain that a phenomenon that is ultimately able to perform a function is the definition of a specifically characterized phenomenon, and a phenomenon that is ultimately unable to perform a function is the definition of a generally characterized phenomenon.

They claim this because the master Devendrabuddhi stated, “These very definitions–being able to perform a function and so on–are to be connected with the specifically characterised and so on [by way of the verse] that begins ‘whatever is ultimately able to perform a function.’”

This is inaccurate.

This text, which states, “That which is ultimately able to perform a function, and so forth,” teaches the definitions of the two truths, not the definitions of the specifically and generally characterized phenomena. This is because Devendrabuddhi did not state that those things which are able and unable to perform a function are definitions of specifically and generally characterized phenomena; rather, he stated that those things which exist ultimately and those which exist conventionally are explained as the specifically and generally characterized phenomena.

For that very text states:

“Those things that are to be apprehended as existing conventionally and ultimately are explained as specifically and generally characterized phenomena. That which exists ultimately is specifically characterised, and that which exists conventionally is generally characterised.”

According to our own system, the definitions of the two— specifically characterised and generally characterised —are as follows: the definition of specifically characterised is that which is established from its own side, rather than being merely designated by the conceptual cognition; and the definition of generally characterised is a phenomenon that is established merely as a designation by the conceptual cognition.

**Second, the etymological explanation:**

The Pramāṇavārttika (Chapter 1) states:

“The cognition which, based on distinct things,
has the appearance of a single object,
and which by its own form
conceals the form of others.

By that concealer, though things are distinct,
their very distinctness is concealed,
[and] things, by means of some form,
appear as though non-distinct.”

Commentary: The “conventional” in the term “conventional truth” refers to a conventional cognition, which is a conceptual cognition in which natures appear as mixed. It is called “conventional” because that conceptual cognition obstructs the seeing of the specifically characterised by taking it as an apprehended object. For example, relying on individual instances of trees, which are the reverse of what is not a tree, the mind apprehending the universal “tree,” in which individual trees appear as a single type/kind, takes that own-form, the word-object, as its apprehended object. In this way it is as if it obscures the seeing of the other-form of individual instances of trees which would have been taking the specifically characterised as the apprehended object.

The reason that, for that concealing cognition, the individual trees appear as though they are not distinct even though they are distinct in their own nature is the following. [First], because of the certain form of the instances of trees that are established as being the reverse of non-trees, and, [second] because, by taking that very word-object “tree” as an apprehended object, it conceals the seeing of the instances of trees as being distinct in their own nature. The manner in which that form of that reverse from non-trees is the seed for the instances of trees appearing as a single type will be explained below.

With the first four lines of the text, having identified the conventional cognition that serves as the basis for the etymological explanation of the term “conventional” in “conventional truth,” the remaining four lines identify the way the generality which is the appearing object appears to that conventional cognition. Because that very meaning generality of a tree is true from the perspective of how it appears to that conventional cognition, it is termed “conventional truth.”

The “ultimate” in the term “ultimate truth” is an ultimate cognition, which is a non-erroneous direct perception that takes specifically characterised phenomena as its appearing object. It is termed “ultimate” because, being an awareness that is free from conceptual cognition and non-erroneous, it is the best form of cognition. Because something is true from the perspective of how it appears to that [cognition], it is an ultimate truth. For example, a pot.

**The third part, the explanation of the meaning derived from that, has three sections:** establishing that the appearance of conceptual cognition does not exist in the ultimate sense, explaining exclusion-of-others (*anyāpoha, gzhan sel*), and explaining the object of verbal convention.

**The first, establishing that the appearance of conceptual cognition does not exist in the ultimate sense**, is as stated in the Pramāṇavārttika (Chapter 1):

“It is from the perspective of that [cognition],
that a generality is proclaimed to exist;
The way that it is imputed by that [cognition],
does not ultimately exist.

The instances do not extend [into one another],
and no separate extending thing appears.”

Commentary: Take the subject, the appearance of a tree in the way that it is imputed by the cognition of the generality/generic tree: it follows that it does not ultimately exist, because it does not exist as substantially either one with or different from the specifically characterised instances. It does not exist as substantially one with the specifically characterised instances, because the instances do not extend into one another. It does not exist as substantially different from them, because a substantially separate generality that extends to the particulars does not appear to an un-mistaken cognition.

If that appearance of a tree were to exist ultimately, then the cognition of the generality/generic tree would be unmistaken with respect to its appearing object. If that were the case, it would follow that the individual instances of trees would extend into one another, because the individual instances of trees appear to the cognition of the generality/generic tree as sharing one nature that is mixed and because that cognition of a generality is an unmistaken cognition.
The manner in which they appear to have a shared nature is posited as follows: when the meaning generality of a tree appears to the conceptual mind as extending to both a Sheesham tree and a juniper tree, the generality/generic tree that is of the nature of the Sheesham tree appears as the generality/generic tree that is of the nature of the juniper tree, and the generality/generic tree that is of the nature of the juniper tree appears as the generality/generic tree that is of the nature of the Sheesham tree.

Furthermore, does the object of apprehension of a cognition of the generality of a tree appear to it as a whole (possessing parts) or not? If the former, then because for any cognition to which a coarse whole appears, the parts and the whole must appear as having different aspects, it would follow that the parts and the whole are different substances, because from the perspective of an unmistaken cognition of a generality, the generality and the individual instances appear individually, like the moon and the stars. If the aspect of the object as a whole does not appear to that cognition of a generality, then it would follow that the tree and the individual instances of trees would not appear individually, like water mixed with milk, and thus it would follow that the individual instances end up being substantially one.

Pramāṇavārttika (Chapter 1):

“Therefore this conceptual mind,
which apprehends things to share a single nature, is wrong.
Its seeds are [the particulars] that bear the name and its meaning,
which are distinct from one another.”

Commentary: Therefore this conceptual mind, which grasps the things, the individual trees, as sharing a single nature is wrong, that is, mistaken. The cause of the error is the very imprint left by direct perception that sees those individual trees. The seeds, or basis of the error, by seeing which that imprint is laid down, are the specifically characterised instances of trees, that bear the name and meaning “tree”. These exist as distinct from one another, that is, unmixed. For that reason it was said: “[and] things, by means of some form, appear as though non-distinct”.

Regarding the statement, “ It is from the perspective of that [cognition], that a generality is said to exist;,” someone asks: “Does that which appears to a conceptual mind apprehending a tree as the reverse of non-tree constitute a generality that extends onto the specific instances of trees, or does it not? If it is the former, then it must be that the specifically characterised trees appear to the conceptual mind as the reverse of non-tree. If that were the case, it contradicts the impossibility of something specifically characterised  becoming an appearing object of a conceptual mind. If that which appears to the conceptual mind as the reverse of non-tree is not a generality of those specifically characterised specific instances of trees, then it follows that the conventions such as ‘generality’ and ‘common locus’ and so forth applied to ‘tree’ and ‘Sheesham’ are meaningless.”

Even though specific instances of trees appear to the conceptual mind as the reverse of non-tree, the fault does not follow that one has to accept specifically characterised as the appearing object of the conceptual mind. This is because the appearance of the reverse of non-tree to the conceptual mind is its appearing object, and that does not exist ultimately, but is a phenomenon that is merely designated by the conceptual mind. Although generalities, common loci, and so forth do not exist ultimately, the conventions of “generality,” “common locus,” and so forth is established on the basis of what appears to the conceptual mind.

(Regarding the nature of that conceptual mind apprehending a tree:) The Pramāṇavārttika (Chapter 1) states:

“The conceptual cognition apprehending that nature,
though it does not have an [actual] object, appears as though it does.
It arises from non-causes of it [tree],
and culminates in [the particulars].”

Commentary: That conceptual mind apprehending a tree, which is induced by a direct perception of a tree, possesses three characteristics: the characteristic of its nature, which is that although it does not have a specifically characterised tree as its own apprehended object, it appears as though it were its apprehended object; the characteristic of its cause, in that it arises indirectly from the specifically characterised trees, which is distinct from the object of “non-tree” that is not the result of that tree; and the characteristic of result, in that it is the culmination of attaining the specifically characterised tree.

(Regarding the features of how the objects appear to the conceptual mind)

The Pramāṇavārttika (Chapter 1) states:

“The form which appears to that as one with the external,
and as if reversed from others,
is not actually [a tree], because it lacks
the factors in being examined.”

The commentary states: Regarding that conceptual mind apprehending a tree, the way the object appears to it is that the meaning generality of the tree appears as if it were the form of a tree, as if it were one with an external tree, and as if it were the reverse of not tree. However, whatever appears in that way is not actually a tree, because, it lacks the factors examined by one who seeks a tree, namely the ability to fulfil the purpose of a tree.

(Regarding the way in which the terminology of generalities, common loci, and so forth is applied)

If one objects, “If the appearance to the conceptual mind of the reverse of non-tree does not exist as a functional thing, then it is not tenable to apply the convention of a “generality of instances of tree” to that (appearance), nor to apply the convention of “common locus of tree and Sheesham” to that (appearance of the reverse of non-Sheesham),” (we respond as follows).

The Pramāṇavārttika (Chapter 1) states:

“Those objects that exist for cognition
are of the nature of such isolates.
Therefore, they appear as if they were not different,
and as if they were the reverse of others.

With respect to these, the cognitions and expressions
that engage in generalities and
common loci, apply conventions
that have mistaken objects.”

Commentary: Because instances of specifically characterised trees are of the nature of being reversed from non-tree, they appear to the conceptual mind apprehending a tree as if they were not different in kind/from the typology “tree”. Therefore, it is on the basis of that appearance that one applies the term “generality” of instances of trees. Furthermore, because the Sheesham itself appears as the reverse of non-tree in general, and as the reverse of non-Sheesham in particular, one applies the term “common locus” to that appearance. If one asks what kind of conceptual mind is it that applies such terminology of generalities and common loci, it is a mistaken, i.e., erroneous, cognition.

If one objects, “If the cognition that applies the terminology of generalities and common loci has a mistaken object, then it is inconsistent that a specifically characterised tree can be attained by relying upon it,” (we respond as follows).

Pramāṇavārttika (Chapter 1):

“All those [conceptions] of things
rely on [what is] not [mixed] with one another.
Therefore those that have exclusion-of-other as their object
and are related to the [real] thing,
are the basis for attaining the thing.
This is as was explained for inference.
Though alike in being mistaken, others cannot [attain the thing],
just as a jewel cannot be [attained] from the light of a lamp.”

Commentary: There is no inconsistency. All those conceptions that make conventions of generalities and common loci for things are conventions that accord with reality, because they rely indirectly on instances that are not mixed with one another. Conceptions that are indirectly related to the thing that is their determined object are the basis for attaining the thing. This is just as was explained earlier, that inference based on effect and nature reasons does not deceive with respect to the specifically characterised. Other superimposing cognitions, though alike in being mistaken with respect to their appearing object, cannot lead to attaining their object of engagement. The example for these two is apprehending a jewel in the light of a jewel leads to obtaining the jewel, but apprehending the light of a lamp as a jewel does not.

(The reasons for applying those Conventions) Pramāṇavārttika (Chapter 1):

“With respect to those that are many of the same effect,
which rely on [something] other and are not effect-possessors of that,
words and cognitions
apply conventions [such] as “one”.
Likewise [they] make even one thing many.
In order to indicate fully indicate that as something,
because of being different from not effect-possessors,
various dharmas are known.”

Commentary: There is a reason for the application of the convention “tree” to many instances of trees (that are effects of the same kind/type, because they are not than effect-possessors of that tree while they rely on something other that is tree) that is because they are the reverse of not tree. Likewise, there is a reason for the application of the convention “common locus” to a single Sheesham tree (because valid cognition knows it to have dharmas that are various isolates of “different from non-tree that is not an effect-possessor of that tree”, and also “being different from non-Sheesham”) and it is that, in general it is reversed from not tree and, in particular, it is reversed from not Sheesham. The purpose of applying the convention “many”, I.e., common locus, to Sheesham is to fully indicate it as something that is tree and Sheesham.

(The reason why those presentations do not exist ultimately) If one asks, what is the reason that those conventions of “common loci” and “generality” have mistaken objects?
Pramāṇavārttika (Chapter 1):

“This word-object and the common locus,
though they do not exist,
they are described in accordance with common usage;
in the real thing, this is impossible.”

Commentary: This word-object, the generality and the common locus, which appear as a single isolate and as a combination of two, cannot be established in the thing. For although it does not exist as a thing in the way it appears to the conceptual mind, it is described in accordance with common usage.

(The purpose of making those presentations) Pramāṇavārttika (Chapter 1):

“The presentations of dharma (*dharma, chos*) and dharma-possessor (*dharmin, chos can*),
of things such as different and non-different,
are without examining the meaning of suchness,
[and are based] on how they are commonly known in the world.

Relying on just that,
all [presentations of] the probandum and proof are set forth.
It is in order to enter the ultimate,
that they are made by the wise.”

Commentary: The presentation of dharma and dharma-possessor, [the presentation of] things such as “common locus” that are different, and things such as “generality” which are non-different: these are set forth, without examining the meaning of the suchness of things. [Instead, they are presented] in reliance on how they are commonly known to the conceptions of the world. And all presentations of a probandum and [its] proof are set forth in reliance on just how [things] appear to the conceptual mind. All this has a purpose which is that it was made by the wise in order to enter the ultimate.

The valid cognition that initially realizes the sixteen aspects of the Four Truths such as the impermanence and so on is inference. Because one must rely upon a reason for that to arise, it is necessary to make numerous distinctions regarding the probandum and the proof, dharma and dharma-possessor, generality and common locus, and so forth. Although the self-isolates of generality, common locus, the probandum, the proof, and so forth are conceptual designations, their illustrations are specifically characterised. Therefore, because holding onto generic dharmas facilitates the understanding of things that are hidden phenomena, they are used as methods for realizing the both immediate and the final goals and are thus not without purpose.

(Generalities and Common Loci cannot be specifically characterised) Pramāṇavārttika (Chapter 1):

“Ultimate things are,
of themselves, neither mixed nor different.
With regard to them, [their being] one in nature
and many is a corruption by the mind.”

Commentary: To that conceptual mind, the appearances of individual instances of trees appearing as having one nature, and tree and the instances of trees appearing as many in nature, are corrupted by mistaken cognition and merely designated. This is so because ultimate things—specifically characterised instances of trees—remain different in nature, without their natures mixing; and tree and the specifically characterised instances are not of different natures, they abide as one nature.

(Summarizing the meaning of those points) Pramāṇavārttika (Chapter 1):

“Regarding the generality and specifics,
This distinction is from the perspective of cognition;
With respect to that very [thing], through isolation from others,
distinctions of dharmas are conceived.”

Commentary: This appearance of distinctions such as “generality” and “specfics” exists only from the perspective of cognition, not in the thing itself. However, it is appropriate to distinguish multiple isolates—such as “produced” and “impermanent”—upon that very tree. This is because the tree is reversed from being “unproduced” and “permanent” in general, and in particular it is reversed from being “not-tree”.

**Second, the explanation of exclusion-of-other has two parts:** the natural state of the object (thing) and the explanation of the manner in which that cognition known that object.

The first part, the explanation of the natural state of the object (thing), is as follows: Some say, “If ‘produced’ and ‘impermanent’ are of one essence, then because those two are not different, it follows that the reason produced proving that sound is impermanent is a non-established reason. Conversely, if ‘produced’ and ‘impermanent’ are of different natures, then being produced would be contradictory to being impermanent, and with a contradictory pervasion, [the reason] becomes unestablished.”

The reason why this doubt arises is as follows: When one negates that the appearance of a pot to the conceptual mind as being specifically characterised, one [feels like that] also negates that a specifically characterised pot appears to the conceptual mind, and thus one does not know how to posit it. When one establishes that there is a specifically characterised appearance of pot to the conceptual mind, even the appearance of a pot to the conceptual mind being specifically characterised will not be negated, and thus one does not know how to posit it as merely conceptually designated. Furthermore, if one negates that generally characterised phenomena are functional things, one also negates the functional thing itself and will not know how to posit it; and if one accepts that it is a functional thing, one thinks that the appearance to the conceptual mind must also be accepted as a functional thing. This is the reason why the doubt arises, and this is the central point in this system that is difficult to understand.

Regarding the fact that “produced” and “impermanent” are established as having the same nature but different isolates, it must be established that the aspect of their having different isolates is an imputation. In this regard, it must be established that the appearing object of the conceptual mind that distinguishes those two as being different isolates is not a functional thing. For, if it is not refuted that the appearing object of the conceptual mind is specifically characterised, there is no way to refute that words and the conceptual minds engage [with objects] collectively (*sgrub pas ’jug pa*). Therefore, one must first refute that things such as the appearance of a pot to a conceptual mind are functional things. Furthermore, because non-Buddhists assert that words and the conceptual minds engage [with objects] collectively due to their asserting that specifically characterised [phenomena] are the appearing object of a conceptual mind, they are unable to posit a flawless presentation of valid cognition and objects of comprehension for phenomena that are merely designated by the conceptual mind.

Word-object (*śabdārtha, sgra don*), generally characterised (sāmānyalakṣaṇa, spyi mtshan), appearing object of a conceptual mind and exclusion-of other (*anyāpoha, gzhan sel*) are synonyms.

The appearance of pot to a conceptual mind is the appearing object of a conceptual mind, yet itself it is not a pot. Nevertheless, pot does appear directly to a conceptual mind. Also, if a pot appears to the conceptual mind, it need not be its appearing object not the word-object. These should be understood as the unique features of this system.

There is no fault of the [reason] “produced” being a non-established [reason] in proving that sound is impermanent, because although “produced” and “impermanent” are of the same nature, they are different isolates.

As the Pramāṇavārttika (Chapter 1) states:

“Because all things, by their very nature,
Abide in their own respective natures,
They depend upon isolation
From both similar and other things.”

Commentary: Take all [functional] things, they depend upon isolation from similar classes of things and from others—dissimilar classes of things—because, not being merely designated by the conceptual mind, they naturally abide in their own natures, unmixed with others. In the case of sound, for example, by considering [the class of] produced [phenomena], it is shown that [sound] arises from its causes as a nature that is unmixed with [dharmas] such as permanent phenomena that [belong] to the dissimilar class “unproduced”. With respect to the similar class, [its nature] is unmixed with things such as pots [that belong to the similar class “produced”]. In this way the reason for why the production and impermanence of sound are one nature (substance) with sound is explained.

As the Pramāṇavārttika (Chapter 1) states:

“Therefore, based on what
something is isolated is the reason
that classes are differentiated.”

Commentary: Because sound itself is born from its causes as being reversed from what is not sound, from what is permanent, i.e., unproduced, and so forth, it is explained that it is suitable to divide many classes of isolates based on whatever things it is reversed from such as not sound and permanent that is unproduced. Here, the mention of the word “class” in “that classes are differentiated” is for the purpose of showing that the differentiation of functional things being different is not a differentiation of their substance (\**dravya, rdzas*). These verses show how, although the three—sound, produced, and impermanent—are one in nature, it is not contradictory for their isolates to be different. Also, because they explain that the differentiation of those three is merely designated by the conceptual mind, it is a conventional mode of existence. By showing that their being one in nature is of its substance (rdzas), the ultimate mode of existence of the three is explained.

Pramāṇavārttika (Chapter 1):

“Through these distinctions,
they are fashioned so they will come to be known.”

Commentary: Through these distinctions that differentiate the three, sound, produced, and impermanent, as being one entity and different isolates, the three—sound, produced, impermanent—are known sequentially. Therefore there is no fault that ‘produced’ is not a correct reason for proving that sound is impermanent.

**Second, the explanation of the manner in which that cognition knows that object has two parts:** demonstrating that words and conceptual cognitions are eliminative engagers, refuting that words and conceptual cognitions engage only collectively, and establishing that they engage in exclusions-of-other.

**The first, demonstrating that words and conceptual cognitions are eliminative engagers**, is as follows:
Pramāṇavārttika (Chapter 1)

“Therefore, whichever distinction
is made known by whichever dharma,
that cannot be [made known] by another;
hence [they] are established as different.”

Commentary: Because sound, produced, and impermanent are different isolates, a single thing has as many isolates as there are things/parts/aspects it is reversed from. Whichever particular instance of an isolate is made understood by whichever name-dharma, such as “produced,” cannot be made understood by any other name-dharma. Therefore the meanings of all words are established as different. And because the meanings of words abide as different, the appearing objects of the conceptual mind apprehending them are also established as different.

To summarize the meaning of these three verses: Based on the fact that sound itself abides in a manner that is reversed from unproduced and permanent, using the terms “produced” and “impermanent” [in relation to it] is why the isolates of “produced” and “impermanent” are different. Furthermore, because the application of the term “produced” to sound occurs by way of excluding what is unproduced, it is capable of directly making the isolate of “produced” known, but it is not capable of directly making the isolate of “impermanent” known. Thus, all words and conceptual cognitions are explained to engage [with their objects] by way of exclusion.

(As the Khedrup Tikchen states: “Because these three verses possess the power to demonstrate an extremely vast subject matter and are therefore of great significance, they should not be explained in a superficial manner, but should be explained in detail by those who possess wisdom.”)

The definitions of the two, eliminative engagers (*sel ’jug*) and collective engagers (s*grub ’jug*), are as follows: eliminative engager is that which engages its object by distinguishing it into parts; collective engager is that which engages without distinguishing [it into parts]. Alternatively, collective engager is that which takes its object not by eliminating what the object is not, but by the thing’s nature casting its aspect to it. Eliminative engager is that which engages its object by eliminating what it is not instead of through the power of the thing.

Still another way of defining them is, respectively: engaging its object through the power of the thing or through the power of a linguistic sign (*brda*). The meaning of engaging its object by distinguishing it into parts is, for instance, in the case of sound, that when it engages with a given object, it does not engage with all the parts that are substantially one sharing coming-to-be and persisting with it, but rather engages with each one individually.

**Second, refuting that words and conceptual cognitions engage only collectively:** Because objects are of two kinds, negative and positive phenomena, the object-possessors, words and conceptions, are also of those two kinds. There is therefore a strong likelihood of the doubt: “Are the words and conceptions that are object-possessors of positive phenomena collective engagers?” To refute this:

In the Svavṛtti (Auto-Commentary), it is stated:

“Again, if it is asked how it is known that words and reasons make one understand an elimination (*vyavacchedaḥ*, *rnam par bcad pa*) and do not make one understand, by way of affirmation, the very nature of the thing? It is because other valid cognitions and other words are employed.”

If words and conceptions engaged [their objects] collectively, it would be pointless for another reason and inference to be employed in order to make known the production, impermanence, and so on of sound that were not known. And it would also be pointless for another reason and inference to be employed in order to make ascertained what has not been ascertained. The first point is established, because...

Pramāṇavārttika (Chapter 1):

“When a single nature of a thing
is itself directly perceived,
what other unseen part
remains for valid cognitions to investigate?”

Commentary: If words and conceptions engaged [their objects] collectively, the conceptual mind apprehending sound would have to be a direct perception of sound. So, when that single nature of the thing was seen, everything that is of its nature would be seen. What other part, then, to be investigated would there be for later valid cognitions to make seen that was unseen by the earlier valid cognitions? There would be none.

The consequence taught by this text is: Regarding the subject sound, it follows that [further] valid cognitions realizing the production and impermanence of sound other than the valid cognition that apprehends sound are pointless, because the conceptual mind engages its object collectively.

Regarding the subject sound, it follows that the words expressing its “produced” and “impermanent” nature other than the word expressing it, are pointless, because the expressive word engages its object collectively. The autonomous [syllogism] argument implied by this is: Regarding the subject sound, the words and conceptual minds that engage it are eliminative object-possessors, because it is meaningful for it to be engaged by other valid cognition and words other than the valid cognition and word that engages it. The “other valid cognition” refers to the proving valid cognition—the reason—and the “valid cognition that engages it” refers to inference.

If one asks: “If sound is seen by direct perception, why must its ‘produced’ and ‘impermanent’ nature also be seen by direct perception?”

The Svavṛtti says:

“Because it is not appropriate to see one part of that which has no parts”. For something that cannot be divided into [different] substantial parts, such as “this is the substance of sound” and “this is the substance of the impermanence of sound”, it is not possible for direct perception to perceive only sound without [also] perceiving the impermanence of sound.

Whether there is a difference or not between being substantially one (*rdzas gcig*), substantially one sharing coming-to-be and persisting (*grub bde rdzas gcig*), and substantially one sharing coming-to-be and persisting from the perspectives of place, time and nature (*yul dus rang bzhin gang la ltos nas kyang grub bde rdzas gcig*) needs to be investigated.

“Coming-to-be and persisting” is, as Śākyabuddhi states:

“‘Coming-to-be’ refers to being connected to a dharma that has not yet been obtained, ‘persisting refers to the continuing of what has been obtained.”

Thus, because at the time of a pot’s cause, a pot is arising but has not [yet] been obtained, and at the time of the abiding of a pot it has been obtained, this is explained in terms of arising and abiding. Since if the arising and abiding are one, the ceasing must also be one, it is necessary that, to be substantially one sharing coming-to-be and persisting, it must be substantially one where arising, ceasing, and abiding are one.

If one objects: “The fault of it being meaningless for other valid cognitions and other words to engage with sound applies to you as well, because when direct perception apprehending sound perceives sound, it perceives all of its ‘produced’ and ‘impermanent’ natures as well,” [how do you respond]?

Pramāṇavārttika (Chapter 1):

“If, through seeing a similarity of visible form,
as with the appearance of silver in mother-of-pearl,
[the mind] did not, owing to a cause of error,
attribute other qualities ...”

Commentary: Just as a mind that mistakes mother-of-pearl for silver arises because of the similarity of the visible property of whiteness, so too, if other qualities such as permanence were not attributed to sound owing to a cause of error, [other valid cognitions and words] would not be needed. But such superimposition is observed, so it is meaningful for other valid cognitions and other words to be employed in order to reverse it.

(The root text says: “Even with what is apprehended by direct perception, which is free of distinctions and parts, for whichever distinction there is a condition for ascertaining, that comes to be ascertained.”)

(Summary of the meaning of the two preceding and following verses)
Pramāṇavārttika (Chapter 1):

“Therefore, when a thing is perceived,
all its qualities are indeed perceived;
but because of error they are not ascertained,
and so proof is employed.”

Commentary: Sound along with its production and impermanence are one, without any division into separate substantial parts. Therefore, by directly perceiving the thing, sound, all its qualities, such as its production and impermanence, are indeed perceived. Even so, because of a cause of error, one does not ascertain them as they have been perceived, so a reason and inference proving that sound is impermanent must be employed. The first two lines summarize the meaning of the preceding verse, and the last two summarize the meaning of the following verse.

The second [it would also be pointless for another reason and inference to be employed in order to make ascertained what has not been ascertained] is established, because:

Pramāṇavārttika (Chapter 1):

“If inference too grasped [its object] by the power of the thing,
then, since one dharma would be ascertained,
it would grasp all dharmas. For [the proponent of] exclusion,
this fault does not follow.”

Commentary: [It follows that] it would be pointless for another reason and inference to be employed in order to make ascertained what has not been ascertained, because the conceptual mind apprehending sound would be an ascertaining consciousness that engages sound collectively. The pervasion holds because, if an ascertaining consciousness ascertained its object collectively, the cognition ascertaining sound would have to ascertain all the dharmas of sound. If it took sound as its appearing object and ascertained collectively that very thing, it would have to ascertain it by way of the thing’s nature casting its aspect to it, without depending on eliminating what is not it. In that case, the two parts—sound being ascertained while sound’s production and impermanence not being ascertained—would be contradictory. This is because, the thing itself—sound and its production and impermanence—do not have separate parts.

And not only the awareness ascertaining the basis: even an inference ascertaining a single property, such as the impermanence on sound, would grasp and ascertain all the dharmas of sound, because inference would grasp its object by the power of the thing.

For the proponent of exclusion, this fault does not follow, because each valid cognition cuts off its own superimposition; one does not cut off all.

The root text says:

“As many superimpositions of aspects as there are,
Just so many are the ascertainments and words applied to eliminate them;
Hence, these have distinct objects of engagement.
Otherwise, if a single entity were entirely encompassed
By a single word or cognition,
Nothing else would remain as an object, and therefore
All other terms would turn out to be mere synonyms.”

Pramāṇavārttika (Chapter 1):

“Therefore, in this way, a reason
is proclaimed to be an object-possessor [engaging] by exclusion.
If not, when the subject were established,
what else would remain unestablished by it?”

Commentary: For that reason, it is established that, inference that relies on reasons in this manner is an object-possessor [engaging] by exclusion. If not, when the subject, sound, is established by valid cognition, what other dharmas of sound would remain unestablished by it? There would be none, so all reasons and inferences would become pointless.

**Third, establishing that they engage in exclusion-of-other**. It has two parts: identifying the generality that is exclusion-of-other, refuting that only functional things are the objects of linguistic signs, and establishing exclusion-of-other as the object of linguistic signs.

**First, regarding identifying the generality that is exclusion-of-other:** Someone asks: “When the linguistic sign ‘cow’ is applied to a white-spotted [cow], what is the reason that black humpbacked [cows] and others are understood as cows?” The answer: the object to which the linguistic sign “cow” is applied is the generality cow, and that very generality is present equally in the white-spotted one, the black humpbacked one, and all the rest. If there were no common dharma, a generality cow present in both, then, once the convention “cow” had been applied to the white-spotted one, the cognition thinking “it’s a cow” would not arise later on seeing a black humpbacked one. In the same way, once the convention has been applied to the white-spotted [cow], the awareness thinking “it’s a cow” does not arise upon seeing a horse.

As for identifying the generality cow that extends to both: the Vaiśeṣikas assert a real generality that is a separate functional thing from the instances and pervades all of them. Our own system holds that the exclusion-of-other that is reversed from not being a body with a dewlap etc. is itself the generality cow that is the object of the linguistic sign.

**Second, the refutation of the claim that only functional things are the objects of linguistic signs:**

The Pramāṇavārttika (Chapter 1) states:

“Words indicate that to which a linguistic sign is assigned.
This serves conventions.
At that time, there is no specifically characterised [object];
therefore the linguistic sign does not [apply] to it.”

Commentary: Words indicate the object to which the linguistic sign has been assigned. Since that linguistic sign serves in communicating the object at the time of [using] the convention, if the instance were the object of linguistic sign without relying on elimination what it is not, then that very white-spotted cow from the time of the linguistic sign would have to appear later at the time of [using] the convention. However, at that time, that previous specifically characterised cow has ceased and no longer exists. Therefore, linguistic signs cannot be applied only to specifically characterised things without the exclusion-of-other being the object of linguistic signs.

If the Vaiśeṣikas say, “There is no fault, because a separate generality cow is the object of the linguistic sign,”

Pramāṇavārttika (Chapter 1):

“People engage [with something]
to try and attain it
after they know the functions it can perform.
[With this in mind] one applies expressions to things.
In such a context, the generality cannot perform a functional purpose.”

Commentary: The purpose in applying the linguistic sign “cow” to things such as the white-spotted one as a positive determination, is this: people who seek a cow, knowing that instances of cows are capable of performing functions such as giving milk and carrying loads, act in order to achieve those functions. That purpose is not accomplished by applying the linguistic sign to a generality that is a separate thing, because a separate generality cow cannot perform functions such as giving milk and carrying loads.

Someone says: “The linguistic sign ‘cow’ is applied to an instance of cow that possesses the separate generality cow.”

Reply: If the word “cow” applied to instances of cow by way of their possessing a separate generality, it would follow that the word “cow” does not apply to instances of cow as their actual name. If you accept this, it follows that it applies to them figuratively, because it does not apply as an actual name. If you accept that, it would follow that a common locus of cow and its instance is impossible. If you accept that, it follows that the word “cow” does not apply to instances of cow by way of their possessing a separate generality, because a common locus of cow and its instance is impossible.

As Ācārya Dignāga said:

“Because it is not actual, does not possess it,
because it is figurative, and because it is impossible.”

**Third, establishing exclusion-of-other as the object of linguistic signs:**

The Pramāṇavārttika (Chapter 1) states:

“A word is uttered so that [a person]
will act, having completely avoided what is other than that.
If by it this is not set apart from those,
then how [could that be]?
If it has [the power of] elimination,
is the purpose of words not exhausted by just that?
Then for you,
what would a separate generality do?”

Commentary: Does the word “cow” eliminate non-cows or not? If that word “cow” does not fully set this cow apart from those non-cows, how could applying the linguistic sign have any purpose? For the word “cow” is uttered so that a person seeking a cow will act toward the cow, having completely avoided what is other than it. If this word does have the power to eliminate non-cows, then the purpose of applying words is exhausted by just that, understanding the object while completely avoiding what is other than it. So for you, on the occasion of applying the linguistic sign, what would a separate generality do? There is no need for one.

Pramāṇavārttika (Chapter 1)

“The linguistic sign has as its purpose making that known.”

Commentary: Therefore, unless the linguistic sign is applied to the exclusion-of-other, nothing is tenable as its object: not the specifically characterised instance, not a separate generality that is a functional thing, and not an instance possessing such a generality. So linguistic signs have as their purpose making one know and understand that exclusion-of-other the word-object to which the linguistic sign is applied in this way is the generality that is an exclusion-of-other.

Pramāṇavārttika (Chapter 1):

“[Because] it has the appearance of what does not perform that [function]
but [appears] as though it does perform it,
and has as its seed the mere thing
that is different [from] things,
which gives rise to a cognition without an object.
Because it has the nature of a component that completely
avoids what does not [perform] that [function].
[And] because it depends on different things,
it is held to be non-misleading regarding the object.”

Commentary: Although the generality that is an exclusion-of-other, the object to which the linguistic sign “cow” is applied, is not a functional thing, that linguistic sign is held to be non-misleading regarding the object, cow, for [the following reasons]: (1) it has as its seed the mere cow-thing that is different from non-cow things that gives rise to a conceptual mind apprehending cow without an apprehended object [in as much as] the word-object of cow does not perform the functions of a cow but appears as though it does; (2) because it is of the nature of a causal component for engaging with cows, having completely avoided what does not perform a cow’s function; (3) and because it is a convention that accords with reality, which depends indirectly on cows that are different from non-cow things.

The definition of exclusion-of-other is: that which when it appears to cognition, appears as having the aspect of a negation. It has two divisions: an affirming negation exclusion-of-other and a non-affirming  negation exclusion-of-other.

The Tattvasaṃgraha [Chapter 20] states:

Here, there are two types of exclusions,
affirming negation and non-affirming.

In its commentary Kamalaśīla states: “affirming negation and non-affirming” means the negation that is affirming and non-affirming; that is to say, the definition of an affirming negation and the definition of a non-affirming negation.” Because he states this, the definitions of these two are as follows: an exclusion-of-other such that the word expressing it, in the wake of negating its object of negation, does not imply another dharma: this is the definition of an exclusion that is a non-affirming negation. An exclusion-of-other such that the word expressing it, in the wake of negating its object of negation, does imply another dharma: this is the definition of an exclusion that is an affirming negation.

The latter of these is divided into two. As stated in the Tattvasaṃgraha:

“Affirming negation is also of two types
due to the distinction between cognition and the object.”

These are the cognitive exclusion-of-other and specifically characterised object exclusion-of-other. The definition of the first is: exclusion-of-other that is an affirming negation that is merely designated by the conceptual mind. An illustration of this is the appearance of reverse of non-pot to the conceptual mind. The definition of the latter is: exclusion-of-other that is an affirming negation that is ultimately established through its own characteristics. An illustration of this is reverse of non-pot.

#### 1.2.3 The Cittamātra System

Third, the explanation of the Cittamātra system has three parts: identifying the provisional and definitive [meaning] of the three wheels, the way the first two wheels are interpreted as being of provisional meaning, and the explanation of the definitions of the two truths.

**The first, the provisional and definitive [meaning] of the three wheels**, is as follows. Of the three successive turnings of the wheel of dharma by the Bhagavān, which is of provisional meaning and which is of definitive meaning? And what are provisional and definitive meaning? Ārya Asaṅga and his brother hold that a Sūtra whose meaning must be explained by another Sūtra is of provisional meaning (*neyārtha*, *drang don*), and that the opposite of that—a Sūtra whose meaning need not be explained by another and which can be accepted literally—is of definitive meaning (*nītārtha*, *nges don*).

In the first turning of the wheel, intending to guide those to be trained who are of the Śrāvaka lineage, [the Buddha] primarily taught the four truths as the subject matter according to a presentation in which dharmas such as the aggregates exist through their own characteristics and as external objects. The explanation in the first turning of the wheel that external objects exist is of provisional meaning, because their being established in that way is invalidated by the reasoning that refutes external objects.

In the middle turning of wheel, intending to guide those of the Mahāyāna lineage, the teaching that all dharmas do not have characteristics too is of provisional meaning, because another Sūtra explains that it was with a particular mode of naturelessness in mind that [the Buddha] taught that all dharmas are natureless and so forth.

The way the final turning of the wheel explains that the completely imputed (*parikalpita*, *kun btags*) is not truly existent and that the other-powered (*paratantra*, *gzhan dbang*) and the thoroughly established (*pariniṣpanna*, *yongs grub*) are truly existent is of definitive meaning, because its meaning does not need to be explained by another Sūtra and its literal meaning is not invalidated by reasoning. This presentation is explained in the *Mahāyānasaṃgraha*, the *Bodhisattvabhūmi*, the *Viniścayasaṃgrahaṇī*, the *Mahāyānasūtrālaṃkāra*, the root text and commentary of the *Madhyāntavibhāga*, and elsewhere; and the two Lords of reasoning (Ācārya Dignāga and Dharmakīrti) also hold views in accordance with this presentation of the provisional and the definitive.

**Second, the way the first two wheels are interpreted as being of provisional meaning** has two parts: the way the first wheel is interpreted as being of provisional meaning, and the way the middle wheel is interpreted as being of provisional meaning.

**The first, the way the first wheel is interpreted as being of provisional meaning**, has two parts: presenting the entities of the three natures (*trisvabhāva*, *ngo bo nyid gsum*), and establishing the reality that is the emptiness of duality.

**The first, presenting the entities of the three natures:** What is the other-powered nature? The *Mahāyānasaṃgraha* states:

“What is the definition of the other-powered? That cognisance having the foundation consciousness (*ālayavijñāna*, *kun gzhi rnam shes*) as its seed and subsumed under unreal imagination.”

And:

“The mere cognisance of these cognisers that is subsumed under unreal imagination and is the basis for the appearance of non-existent and erroneous objects—this is the definition of the other-powered.”

Thus it teaches that the consciousnesses that appear as outer and inner dharmas—the bases on which subject and object are conceived as different substances—and the things that are their objects are the other-powered. It is called “other-powered” owing to its feature of being under the power of the imprints of dualistic appearance.

What is the nature of the completely imputed? The *Mahāyānasaṃgraha* states:

“What is the definition of the completely imputed? It is the appearance of mere cognisance as objects, although there are no objects.”

This is said of the way their objects appear as separate to non-conceptual other-cognisers that arise from stable imprints. Why is this appearance completely imputed? Because it is merely designated by the conceptual mind and is not truly established.

These completely imputed [natures] that appear as external objects exist among objects of knowledge, so they are not the object of negation of reasoning. [Something] being established in the way blue appears to an eye consciousness as separate from it is impossible among objects of knowledge; so although that is the completely imputed [nature] that is the object of negation, one must make the distinction that it is not the completely imputed [nature spoken] of in the three natures. For if something is completely imputed of the three natures, it must be a conventional truth, and it is contradictory for something that is not an established base to be a conventional truth.

What is the thoroughly established nature? The *Mahāyānasaṃgraha* states:

“What is the definition of the thoroughly established? It is the complete absence of the characteristics of objects in the very nature of the other-powered.”

Thus it teaches that the thoroughly established is the absence of imputation of subject and object in the very nature of the other-powered.

It is not tenable to explain the thoroughly established that is empty of the duality of subject and object only as emptiness, because it is not a functional thing and so cannot be the explicit object of direct perception, whereas the exalted meditative-equipoise wisdom of Āryas perceives dharmatā directly. The first [reason] is established, because: when describing the assertions of the Cittamātrin, the *Madhyamakāloka* states:

“Thus, to begin with, direct perception does not realise the absence of all functional things, because its object is a functional thing.”

And the second [reason] is established, because the same text states:

“There definitely exists an ultimate functional thing that is individually experienced by Āryas. Otherwise, the *Ārya Saṃdhinirmocana* Sūtra would not refer to a non-functional thing. What is that functional thing? It is that inexpressible [thing] that the exalted wisdom of Āryas and the vision of Āryas has completely and perfectly awakened to, for it is precisely that inexpressible dharmatā that leads to complete and perfect awakening.”

Therefore, the explicit object of meditative equipoise must be explained as a thoroughly established functional thing.

Its illustration is as stated in the *Jñānasārasamuccaya*:

“Consciousness free of subject and object
Exists ultimately.
So it is renowned in the Yogācāra texts
That go beyond the ocean of intellect.”

Accordingly, the immutable thoroughly established—the clear and cognizing aspect of the experience of consciousnesses, untainted by the imprints of dualistic appearances, which conditions cannot transform into anything other than [its] non-dual nature—is the reality that is the emptiness of duality, for the *Pramāṇavārttika* (Chapter 3) states:

“Therefore emptiness of duality
Is the reality of that as well.”

This teaches that cognisance that is empty of the duality of subject and object is the reality of that consciousness. Some say that Śākyabuddhi explains the reality that is the emptiness of duality as self-cognizing awareness (\**svasaṃvedana*, *rang rig*), because this Ācārya states:

“Bodhisattvas, in knowing the selflessness of phenomena, know only self-cognizing awareness empty of duality.”

The definitions of the three natures: a dharma that is an appearance to a consciousness tainted by the imprints of dualistic appearance and that cannot withstand analysis by reasoning that analyses the final [mode of existence] is the definition of the completely imputed. A functional thing that is under the power of the imprints of dualistic appearance is the definition of the other-powered. Clarity and cognisance free of dualistic appearance that is the mode of existence of the consciousness that is one’s own [object of] experience, is the definition of the thoroughly established.

If one asks: what is the underlying intention, the purpose and so forth for teaching external objects in the first turning of the wheel: the underlying intention is as the *Viṃśikā* states:

“From whatever seed of its own
A cognisance arises with whatever appearance,
That [seed] and that source (*āyatana*, *skye mched*)
Were described as being two by the Sage.”

That is, with the underlying intention of the ripening imprints and the appearance of external things, [the Buddha] taught the subject and object as being two. The purpose is as the same text states:

“That the sources of form and so forth exist
Was taught with an intention
For those beings who would be tamed by it,
Like spontaneously born sentient beings.”

The refutation [of the literal reading] is that it is invalidated by the reasonings that refute subject and object as different substances.

**Second, establishing the reality that is the emptiness of duality has two parts:** identifying the object of negation, and explaining the reasoning that refutes it.

**The first, identifying the object of negation.** The *Abhidharma Sūtra* states:

“The consciousness that holds all the seeds
Of all dharmas is foundation consciousness.”

The seed of all outer and inner things is called the foundation consciousness (*ālayavijñāna*, *kun gzhi rnam shes*). From the maturing of the imprints that the six collections of consciousness place on the foundation consciousness as concordant potencies, what arises is simply impure other-powered consciousness. This, then, appears to childish beings as separate inner and outer subjects and objects. Conceiving of subject and object as different substances in accordance with that appearance is grasping at a self of phenomena, and its determined object is the self of phenomena that is the object of negation.

Those who accept external objects explain form and so forth as being established by particles. Among them, the Vaibhāṣikas explain that coarse material dharmas are established upon the aggregation of eight substantial particles—the four primary elements, earth, water, fire and air, and the four derivative elements, visible form, odour, taste and tangibility—and that if material things that have parts are gradually divided into their separate parts, one finally arrives at the subtlest particle. Of the two kinds of these subtlest particles, substantial particles and aggregated particles, the Kashmiri Vaibhāṣikas explain that the substantial subtlest particle is not only the subtlest of all particles and the initial building block of material things, but is also partless and one single [particle] is surrounded by many without any of them touching each other.

The Auto-commentary on the *Abhidharmakośa* states:

“Do the subtlest particles touch one another or not? The Kashmiris say that they do not touch. Why? If they were to touch in their entirety, the substances would merge. If they touched on one side, it would follow that they have parts; but subtlest particles are partless.”

If one objects, “If the subtlest particles do not touch one another, they will fall apart”: they remain without scattering because they are held together by wind, and also because they are mutually connected with one another through the power of the substance.

The same text states:

“Why do aggregations not fall apart when struck? Because they are held by the wind element.”

Likewise, Ācārya Śubhagupta’s *Bāhyārthasiddhi* says:

“Without touching each other,
They abide without parts;
Therefore the earth-maṇḍala and so forth
Arise from aggregation.”

Partless means that its nature cannot be divided into directional parts or [other] parts. If it could be divided, it would follow that the division would never end and that it would not be the smallest. “A single one surrounded by many” means that even a single central subtlest particle is surrounded by other subtlest particles. “Not touching one another” means if they touched on all sides they would merge with one another, and if they touched on one side each it would follow that they have parts; so they are explained as not touching and as having space [between them].

The Sautrāntika Ācārya Bhadanta holds that although subtlest particles do not touch one another, they are without space [between them]. The *Madhyamakālaṃkārapañjikā* states:

“Others say that they neither touch nor have space, but abide only without gaps—as Bhadanta says: ‘The particles do not touch, but because they are without space they are thought of as touching.’”

Thus, although the Vaibhāṣikas and Sautrāntikas are alike in holding that subtlest particles are partless and do not touch, there is a difference as to whether or not they have space.

The entity of the substantial subtlest particle: it is posited as the partless particle that is the smallest unit of form, which is a component of obstructive material things that cannot be destroyed by other material things nor even divided into separate parts by the mind. For example, each of the eight substantial partless particles: the four partless particles of the four primary elements, earth, water, fire and air, and the four partless particles of the derivative elements, visible form, odour, taste and tangibility.

The aggregate subtlest particle is posited as the particle that the mind can divide into separate parts but that cannot be destroyed by other [material things]. It is posited as the smallest aggregate particle that has no smaller aggregated parts. For example, the smallest particle that is a combination of the eight, the four primary elements and the four derivative elements. The *Lakṣaṇānusāriṇī* commentary on the *Abhidharmakośa* states:

“Subtlest particles are of two kinds: substantial subtlest particles and aggregate subtlest particles. Of these, the substantial subtlest particle is the subtlest of all, a component of obstructive form, which neither other forms nor the mind can divide. For that very reason, since it is partless, it is called the smallest unit of form, just as a moment which cannot be halved is the smallest unit of time. The aggregate subtlest particle is the component of a collection of which there is no other subtler aggregation, and which other forms cannot destroy.”

The higher Abhidharma holds that the “subtlest particle” has no end no matter how much it is broken down; rather, it is asserted to be a form possessing parts that appears to the mind as the smallest unit reached when coarse physical form is broken down into parts by the mind as far as the mind is capable of dividing it. The *Abhidharmasamuccayabhāṣya* states:

“Because when broken down there is no end. ‘Having divided it to the smallest unit with the mind, one posits the subtlest particle’ means that however far the mind is able to divide it by breaking it down into parts, to that extent one posits the subtlest particle.”

Ācārya Bhāviveka teaches that not only are material things established from the aggregation of eight substantial particles, but the subtlest particles that are their building blocks also do not abide in isolation; they are aggregations of eight substantial particles and have parts, for the *Tarkajvālā* states:

“Thus, because the subtlest particle has the nature of being a combination of eight substances…”, and “The subtlest particles too are differentiated because of being divided into six directional parts; and the parts too are differentiated by being divided into the six directions.”

The subtlest particle is explained as the subtlest of all particles, as the *Tarkajvālā* states:

“The final unit of subtle form is the subtlest particle,”

and as Ācārya Avalokitavrata’s extensive commentary on the *Prajñāpradīpa* says:

“‘Subtlest particles’ are extremely subtle particles; that beyond which there is no other subtler one is called a subtlest particle.”

Ācārya Āryadeva, Candrakīrti and others teach that subtlest particles are obstructive, since they prevent another subtlest particle from arising in their own location.

The higher Abhidharma and some Mādhyamikas simply do not accept that the subtlest particles that are the initial building blocks of coarse matter are partless; apart from that, their position on subtle and coarse matter does not differ much from the lower Abhidharma. Although they accept external objects, they do not accept external objects as aggregations of partless subtlest particles in the manner of the Vaibhāṣikas and Sautrāntikas.

**Second, the explanation of the reasoning that refutes it has two parts:** the reasoning that refutes partless particles, and the reasoning that refutes subject and object as different substances.

**The first, the reasoning that refutes partless particles.** The *Viṃśikā*:

“If joined simultaneously by six,
The subtlest particle will have six parts.
If the six are in one location,
The mass would be a mere particle.”

Commentary: When six particles—those of the four directions, above and below—join with the central subtlest particle, if they join it at its separate parts, east, west and so forth, the central particle will have six parts and so will lose its partlessness. If they do not join it at separate parts, and the location of one is the very location of the others, then, since all occupy a single location, the mass will be only a single subtlest particle.

If one objects, “There is no fault, because we hold that they abide without touching one another”: The *Viṃśikā*:

“That which has different directional parts
Cannot be one.”

Commentary: If an subtlest particle had no directions, the fault would follow that no particles could be located in its four directions. If it has directions, then any particle that has directions such as east must also possess directional [parts] such as an eastern part,; so holding that particles are partless is untenable. The *Viṃśikā* Auto-commentary states:

“Whether or not one holds that subtlest particles join, ‘that which has different directional parts cannot be one.’ The eastern part of an subtlest particle is different, and so on down to the lower part, which is different too. If the directional parts are different, how can the subtlest particle that is of their nature be a single subtlest particle? ‘How could there be shadow and obstruction?’ If the individual subtlest particles had no different directional parts, how could it be that, when the sun shines on it, shadow falls on one side and not on another? In that case there would be no other side on which the sun[light] does not fall. If one does not accept that directions have different sides, how could one subtlest particle be obstructed by another? If subtlest particles do not have any distinct parts, how could one obstruct another on the side from which the other comes? If there were no obstruction, then, since all would occupy a single location, every aggregate would be a mere subtlest particle.”

The reason: Take the subject, blue, it does not exist as an external object, because its composing partless particles do not exist.

**Second, the reasons that refute subject and object as different substances.**

(1) The *Mahāyānasaṃgraha* states:

“Because, for hungry ghosts, animals, humans
And devas, each according to their kind,
With respect to one thing their minds are different,
Objects are held to be unestablished.”

This teaches the reasoning that different consciousnesses arise with respect to one thing for [different kinds of] beings.

Its explanatory commentary (*Upanibandhana*), composed by the venerable layman [Asvabhāva], explains:

“With respect to the thing that is a river, through the maturation of their own karma, hungry ghosts see it as filled with pus and so forth; animals such as fish regard that same [river] as an abode and dwell in it; humans conceive of it as sweet, clear, cool water, and bathe in it, drink it and enter it. Devas absorbed in the samādhi of the realm of infinite space see it as space, because they have destroyed the perception of form.”

The perceptions of these beings are valid cognitions, but, if a single basis were established by valid cognition as each of these different things, being pus and blood and not being pus and blood would not be contradictory. There could then be no reliability even in what is established by valid cognition; therefore there are no external objects. When the [commentary] speaks of a single common apprehended aspect in saying “the thing that is a river,” this is from an unexamined and unanalysed perspective. When analysed, a single common apprehended aspect is impossible among objects of knowledge.

The reason: Take the subject, wet and moistening water, it does not exist as an external object, because it does not cast its aspect to the sense consciousness to which it appears. The reason is established, because hungry ghosts see it as pus and blood.

The *Pramāṇavārttika* (Chapter 3):

“Even if an external object exists,
It has a determinate nature just as it is experienced,
Not by its own form,
Because of the fault of [its then] having many natures.”

This teaches that blue appears with the aspect of being attractive to some sense consciousnesses that experience blue, and with the aspect of being unattractive to others, by way of a nature determined by their individual imprints; it is not an aspect cast by blue’s own form. Otherwise, there would be the fault that a single blue would abide as having many natures, beautiful and unbeautiful. Therefore, it says, although blue and so forth exist as objects of comprehension, there are no external objects.

The consequence: Take the subject, blue, it follows that it is both attractive and unattractive, because its appearing as attractive or unattractive to the sense consciousnesses to which it so appears is not due to imprints but is an aspect arising from the side of the external object.

(2) The *Mahāyānasaṃgraha* states:

“The past and so forth, likewise in dreams,
And the two kinds of reflection,
Although there are no observed objects,
They are suitable to be observed.”

It teaches that even without an observed object that is an external object, a consciousness that observes it can arise—just as, for someone asleep in a very small room who dreams of a herd of elephants, although the elephants are not established as external objects, there is a consciousness to which they appear. The past and the future become objects [of the mind] merely by being superimposed by mental consciousness; they do not exist in fact.

The reason: Take the subject, the sense direct perception apprehending blue, its apprehended object does not exist as an external object, because it is consciousness. Like a dream consciousness.

(3) The *Mahāyānasaṃgraha* states:

“If objects were established objectively,
Consciousness could not be non-conceptual.
Without that, the attainment of Buddhahood
Would not be tenable.”

It teaches that if objects such as form were established as external objects, that itself would be the mode of existence of form and so forth; in that case ordinary beings would know the state of things directly, and since there would be no error obscuring the state of existence form and so forth, all sentient beings would be liberated without effort.

The reason: Take the subject, form, it does not exist as an external object, because the consciousness to which it appears is mistaken with respect to its state of existence.

(4) The *Mahāyānasaṃgraha* states:

“For bodhisattvas who have attained mastery,
And likewise for yogis,
Through the power of their aspiration,
Entities such as earth and so forth are observed.
In this regard, when those endowed with intelligence
Who have attained the accomplishment of tranquillity,
Bring any dharma to mind,
Those dharmas appear accordingly.
When non-conceptual wisdom is operating,
Because no objects at all appear,
One should understand that there are no objects.”

It teaches that, because, for bodhisattvas and for meditators who have attained mastery over the mind, earth and so forth are observed in whatever way they aspire to through the power of their aspiration; for the yogi that rests in calm abiding who strives for special insight, objects appear merely by directing their attention [to them]; and for the yogi abiding in non-conceptual wisdom, no object appears at all, there are no external objects.

The reason: Take the subject, earth and so forth, they do not exist externally, because the three kinds of consciousness know them in conformity with themselves.

(5) Proof by the reason of “necessary-simultaneous-observation” (*sahopalambhaniyama*, *lhan cig dmigs nges*) that subject and object are not different substances.

The reason of necessary-simultaneous-observation proves that those objects—objects which, if not established as not different in substance from a given consciousness, would have to be accepted as external objects—are not different in substance from the object-possessing consciousness. It does not prove that form and so forth are one substance with every consciousness that has them as its object; otherwise one would have to hold that form and so forth are one substance with omniscient wisdom.

Stating the reason:

The *Pramāṇavārttika* (Chapter 3):

“An object that is necessarily experienced
Simultaneously with the cognition—
By what means could it be established
As other than that?”

And

the *Pramāṇaviniścaya* states:

“Because they are necessarily observed together,
Blue and the cognition of it are not different.”

Accordingly: Take the subject, blue and the sense consciousness to which blue appears, they are not different substances, because they have necessary-simultaneous-observation. Like sound and an object of ear consciousness.

“Simultaneous” means at the same time, “observed” means observed by valid cognition, and “necessary” means pervasion, as Ācārya Dharmottara says:

“‘Simultaneous’ means at the same time; ‘observed’ means realised; ‘necessary’ means pervasion.”

Therefore the meaning of necessary-simultaneous-observation is that two things that are distinct from each other abide together such that the valid cognition observing the one and the valid cognition observing the other cannot be separated.

The way those two valid cognitions abide together inseparably should be understood thus: whenever the one is observed by valid cognition, it is pervaded by the other’s also being observed by valid cognition; and whenever the other is observed by valid cognition, it is pervaded by the first’s also being observed by valid cognition.

The *Pramāṇavārttika* (Chapter 3):

“No object without experience,
Nor any experience without an object,
Is seen to be experienced.”

The pervasion is explained as follows: at the time when the eye consciousness apprehending blue is not experienced by the subjective aspect (*’dzin rnam*), [the self-knower], the eye consciousness apprehending blue does not experience any blue object; and when the eye consciousness apprehending blue does not experience a blue object, that experience—the eye consciousness apprehending blue—is not seen to be experienced by the subjective aspect either.

Proving the pervasion: if two things that are different from each other are such that the valid cognition observing the one and the valid cognition observing the other are inseparable, the pervasion is that they are related. Since being observed together rules out causal relation, they are related as having the same nature. Since it is contradictory for things that mutually have the same nature [with each other] to be separate substances, necessary-simultaneous-observation is pervaded by not being separate substances.

How the counter pervasion is proved:

The *Pramāṇavārttika* (Chapter 3):

“For blue and yellow, which are different,
There is no necessary experience.”

[The counter pervasion] is proven by the reason of of non-observation of what is suitable to be observed: if substantially different dharmas had necessary-simultaneous-observation, it would have to be observed, but it is not. Therefore, whatever is substantially different is pervaded by not having necessary-simultaneous-observation, like with blue and yellow. Blue and the eye consciousness apprehending blue have necessary-simultaneous-observation, and therefore they are not substantially different.

If one asks, “Then why does blue appear to the eye consciousness as separate”: it is because [that consciousness] is tainted by the mistaken imprints of dualistic appearance—just as a mistaken consciousness sees two different moons although there are not two moons.

The *Pramāṇavārttika* (Chapter 3):

“Just as, to a mistaken consciousness, two moons,
Though they do not exist, appear as different.”

If one asks how, if there are no external objects, are the three conditions of a sense consciousness posited? Just as an eye consciousness is ceasing, it lays an imprint on the foundation consciousness immediately afterwards, and from the maturing of that, an eye consciousness arises at a later moment; so the imprint that is the cause of the arising of the eye consciousness is its causal condition, and the potential from which that eye consciousness arises is the governing condition, the eye sense faculty. For Ācārya Vasubandhu’s *Viṃśikā* Auto-commentary states:

“The seed from which arises the cognition that appears as form—which is a particular transformation of its seed—and that which appears, these the Bhagavān taught respectively as the eye and the form source.”

This teaches that the eye sense faculty is posited as that entity that is the potential that exists on the preceding consciousness. Ācārya Dignāga’s

The *Ālambanaparīkṣā* also says:

“Whatever is the nature of the potential
That acts as a cooperative condition, is the sense power.
Furthermore, this does not contradict consciousness.”

The observed-object condition too:

The *Ālambanaparīkṣā* states:

“Whatever is the nature of an inner object of knowledge
That appears as though external
Is the object, because it is of the nature of consciousness
And because it is also a condition.”

And its Auto-commentary states:

“Because the inner consciousness appears as an object and also arises from it; since it possesses these two qualities, only what exists within is the observed-object condition.”

Thus it teaches that blue and so forth, which appear as though external to a sense consciousness, are both that sense consciousness and its object, and are also its condition, and so are posited as the observed-object condition. Blue being “observed” by the sense consciousness is because it is of the nature of what is to be cognized by that. It is its “condition” because the blue appearing to the sense consciousness is an aspect of that single sense consciousness, so that [the consciousness] arises if it exists and does not arise if it does not exist; thus [the consciousness] has a positive and negative concomitance with respect to it.

Even though they are simultaneous, it is tenable as a condition, for the same text states:

“Despite being an aspect, because it is unerring, it is a condition.”

This approach is the assertion of earlier [Ācāryas], such as the Abhidharma Ācārya Vasubandhu.

Ācārya Dignāga’s own position is as follows: In the Auto-commentary he uses the connective “or else,” meaning, in the Vijñaptivāda system, the observed-object condition of a sense consciousness is either as the earlier Ācāryas explain it, or as I shall explain it. The Ācārya’s own assertion is as the *Ālambanaparīkṣā* states:

“Because it deposits the potential, it occurs successively.”

And the Auto-commentary states:

“It also occurs successively: that which appears as an object creates the potential for producing a result similar to itself and [in this way] acts a support of consciousness; so there is no contradiction.”

The meaning is: since each earlier sense consciousness deposits the potential for producing its result, the later sense consciousness, as possessing the aspect of the object, that very potential, which is of the nature of an imprint of similar type on the earlier cognition, will successively [act as] observed-object condition of the later sense consciousness. Such a potential is posited as the observed-object condition not because it is both the observed object of the sense consciousness and its condition, but because it is the principal condition that produces the sense consciousness as possessing the aspect of the observed object. Therefore, whatever is the observed-object condition of a sense consciousness must produce the sense consciousness as possessing the aspect of the observed object, but need not be the observed object of that sense consciousness.

**Second, the way the middle turning of the wheel is interpreted as being of provisional meaning has three parts:** identifying the entities of the three natures, the way the two extremes of superimposition and denial are abandoned, and showing the purpose of knowing the entities of the three natures.

**The first, identifying the entities of the three natures.** If one asks: what the underlying intention in [the Buddha’s] teaching in the middle wheel that all dharmas are natureless?

The *Saṃdhinirmocana* states:

“Paramārthasamudgata, I taught that all dharmas are natureless with these three kinds of naturelessness of dharmas in mind: naturelessness of characteristics, naturelessness of arising and ultimate naturelessness.”

It teaches that he spoke with naturelessness of characteristics (*lakṣaṇa-niḥsvabhāvatā*, *mtshan nyid ngo bo nyid med pa*), naturelessness of arising (*utpatti-niḥsvabhāvatā*, *skye ba ngo bo nyid med pa*) and ultimate naturelessness (*paramārtha-niḥsvabhāvatā*, *don dam pa ngo bo nyid med pa*) in mind.

If one asks, “What is the meaning of the three, the lack of characteristics and so forth?”, the *Saṃdhinirmocana* states: “What is the naturelessness of characteristics of dharmas? It is the completely imputed characteristic. Why? Because the characteristics posited by names and linguistic signs and do not abide through their own characteristics. Therefore it is called naturelessness of characteristics.” It teaches that whatever is imputed is naturelessness of characteristics, and that the reason is that it is merely posited by names and linguistic signs.

The *Viniścayasaṃgrahaṇī* states:

“What is the completely imputed nature to be known as? It is said: the completely imputed nature is known as a mere name.”

The characteristic that the completely imputed lacks is establishment thorugh its own characteristics; and by the very fact that naturelessness of characteristics was taught with reference to the completely imputed, it is established that the other-powered and the thoroughly established exist through their own characteristics.

Again, the *Saṃdhinirmocana* states:

“What is the naturelessness of arising of dharmas? It is the other-powered characteristic of dharmas. Why? Because they arise through the force of other conditions, not by themselves. Therefore it is called naturelessness of arising.”

It teaches that whatever is other-powered has the naturelessness of arising, and that the reason is it is not arise by itself.

Arising by itself is posited as independent arising that does not depend on causes and conditions, for the *Viniścayasaṃgrahaṇī* states:

“Because conditioned phenomena are dependent originations, they arise through the force of conditions; that they do not arise independently is called naturelessness of arising.”

Again, the *Saṃdhinirmocana* states:

“Furthermore, whatever is the thoroughly established characteristic of dharmas is also called ultimate naturelessness. Why? Paramārthasamudgata, whatever is the selflessness of phenomena of dharmas is called their naturelessness; it is the ultimate, and the ultimate is distinguished by being the naturelessness of all dharmas; therefore it is called ultimate naturelessness.”

It teaches that whatever is thoroughly established is the ultimate, and that it is ultimate naturelessness for the reason that it is posited by the mere absence of a self-nature of dharmas. Because this Sūtra teaches that whatever is thoroughly established is pervaded by being suchness that is the negation of a self of phenomena, [the position] that the selflessness of persons is the thoroughly established is ruled out.

The *Viniścayasaṃgrahaṇī* states:

“Because the thoroughly established nature is of one taste everywhere, there is no presentation of distinctions of it.”

A second way of positing ultimate naturelessness is also taught in the *Saṃdhinirmocana* Sūtra:

“What is the ultimate naturelessness of dharmas? Those dharmas that are dependent originations, natureless by virtue of the naturelessness of arising, are also natureless by virtue of ultimate naturelessness. Why? Paramārthasamudgata, whatever is the completely purified observed [object] of dharmas is taught to be the ultimate; the other-powered characteristic is not such a completely purified observed object; therefore it is called ultimate naturelessness.”

It teaches that the other-powered is ultimate naturelessness, for the reason that it is not the observed object of the completely purified path.

The definitions of the three natures: what is merely designated by the conceptual mind is the definition of the completely imputed. What is produced through the power of its causes is the definition of the other-powered. The final observed object that leads to the purification of obscurations when observed and meditated upon, is the definition of the thoroughly established.

**Second, the way the two extremes of superimposition and denial are abandoned has two parts:** identifying the two extremes of permanence and annihilation, and specifically refuting the extreme of superimposition.

**The first, identifying the two extremes of permanence and annihilation**. Denial [is described] in the *Bodhisattvabhūmi*:

“Denying and destroying, by [saying] ‘it does not exist at all in any way,’ the real thing that exists ultimately with an inexpressible nature and is the basis and support of the signs of designating words.”

And the *Viniścayasaṃgrahaṇī* also states:

“The extreme of denial is the denial of the characteristics of the other-powered nature and the thoroughly established nature that do exist, by saying ‘they do not exist.’”

It teaches that holding the other-powered and the thoroughly established, which exist ultimately, to be non-existent is denial. The determined object of such conception is the extreme of denial.

The way it is abandoned: in the *Viniścayasaṃgrahaṇī*, the reason is asked why, while observing that it is established through its own characteristics, one says that the other-powered does not exist. If one answers, “Because it is something observed by an erroneous [mind],” [the text asks]: does that erroneous mind exist through its own characteristics or not? If the first, [your position] that the other-powered is natureless of characteristics is not accurate. If the second, it is not appropriate to say, “Because it is observed by an erroneous [mind], it does not exist through its own characteristics,” because the erroneous [mind] itself does not exist. In this way it refutes [the position] by examining whether the mistaken cognition exists ultimately or not. Those who hold the opposing position in the refutation of [the extreme] denial are those who hold the tenets of the Mādhyamikas, who say that dharmas do not exist through their own characteristics.

Superimposition [is described] in the *Bodhisattvabhūmi*:

“Whatever conception superimposes onto dharmas such as form a self-characterised nature of the words designating things such as form, which does not exist.”

The *Viniścayasaṃgrahaṇī* also states:

“Whatever conception [conceives] the other-powered nature and the thoroughly established nature as the completely imputed nature should be known as the extreme of superimposition.”

It teaches that the conceptual mind that holds the completely imputed to be established through its own characteristics on the other-powered and the thoroughly established is superimposition.

Identifying the completely imputed, the *Saṃdhinirmocana* states:

“Whatever entity-characteristic such as ‘form’ or particular characteristic is posited by names and linguistic signs upon objects experienced by the conceptual mind, upon the basis of the completely imputed characteristics, and upon conditioned marks—or alternatively, whatever entity-characteristics or particular characteristics such as ‘arising of the form aggregate,’ or ‘ceasing of the form aggregate,’ or ‘abandon and thoroughly realise the form aggregate’ are posited by names and linguistic signs—are completely imputed characteristics.”

This is said of the entity imputed by names and linguistic signs as the entity, “this is form,” and as particulars, “this is the arising of form,” and so forth. Since just this much exists on the aggregates and so forth, holding them to exist is not superimposition. Holding the aggregates and so forth to exist through their own characteristics as these entities is superimposition.

That itself must be taken as the grasping at a self of phenomena, because the *Saṃdhinirmocana* states:

“Objects experienced by the conceptual mind, the basis of the completely imputed characteristics, and conditioned marks are thoroughly not established as completely imputed characteristics. Only the non-existence of that very entity is the non-existence of the self of phenomena. That completely pure observed [object] is the thoroughly established characteristic.”

By teaching that the non-establishment, through its own characteristics, of the nature that is completely imputed on form and so forth as entity and particular is the selflessness of phenomena, it implicitly teaches that grasping at the completely imputed entities-and-particulars [imputed] on form etc. to exist through their own characteristics is grasping at a self of phenomena.

Therefore it is not correct to explain the grasping at a self of phenomena only as the grasping of subject and object to be substantially different.

Although the *Bodhisattvabhūmi* does not explicitly state in words that grasping the completely imputed to exist ultimately is superimposition, the *Viniścayasaṃgrahaṇī* clearly explains that the completely imputed does not exist ultimately; so the grasping that is the opposite of that must be explained as superimposition.

The *Viniścayasaṃgrahaṇī* states:

“Things named ‘form’ and so forth—the observed objects of a consciousness that is grounded on names [and is] thoroughly habituated to verbal expression—the way they exist with the nature of form etc. is neither substantial nor ultimate.”

That the thing named “form” is the completely imputed is made clear in that very text for the *Viniścayasaṃgrahaṇī* states:

“Here, the completely imputed nature, which is the observed object of a consciousness that relies on names [and is] thoroughly habituated to verbal expression…”

Existing ultimately, here, is posited as existing through its own characteristics, rather than being posited through the power of conventions. That being so, the first nature does not exist ultimately, and the last two natures do exist ultimately. If one asks how is it that the *Viniścayasaṃgrahaṇī* teaches that signs and conceptual [minds] exist conventionally? Existing conventionally here is posited—in contrast to the *Saṃdhinirmocana*’s explaining the observed object of the completely purified path as the ultimate—in the sense that observing it gives rise to afflictions, for the *Viniścayasaṃgrahaṇī* states:

“Should signs be said to exist conventionally or ultimately? They should be said to exist conventionally, for two reasons: because they give rise to afflictions and because they are the basis of designation.”

If the meaning of “the completely imputed does not exist through its own characteristics” were explained as meaning that the completely imputed does not exist at all, the completely imputed would not be possible among objects of knowledge; and in that case it would contradict the *Saṃdhinirmocana* where it says,

“Those who view the other-powered characteristics and the thoroughly established characteristics as without characteristics deny the completely imputed characteristics too; therefore they are called those who deny all three characteristics.”

The *Viniścayasaṃgrahaṇī* also states:

“If one asks what fault would follow if the completely imputed nature did not exist, it is said: on the other-powered nature there would be no conventional [designation] nor would there be any clinging to the conventional [designation]; without that, there would be neither affliction nor purification.”

If one thinks, as the Sautrāntikas do, that [not existing through its own characteristics] means not existing as a functional thing able to perform a function, since the thoroughly established is explained as existing through its own characteristics, the fault would follow that it is a functional thing; so it must be explained as meaning not truly existent. Truly established, existing ultimately and established through its own characteristics are synonymous.

**Third, specifically refuting the extreme of superimposition has three parts:** identifying the object of negation, explaining the reasoning that refutes it, and how one enters the cognisance-only (*rnam rig tsam*) by means of these reasonings.

The first, identifying the object of negation. If one knows how form and so forth, completely imputed as entity and as particular, are posited through the power of names and linguistic signs, one will easily understand the grasping at a self of phenomena that holds the opposite of that. So I shall explain how the completely imputed is posited through the power of names and linguistic signs. When the *Mahāyānasaṃgraha* explains the characteristics of the other-powered, it teaches fifteen cognitions and explains that the first nine arise from the imprints of verbal expression, the cognitions of the distinction between self and other arise from the imprints of the view of self, and the last four arise from the imprints of the branches of existence.

Of the first nine cognitions, the fourth, the cognition of what is experienced (*nye bar spyad pa’i rnam rig*), is taught to be the six constituents (*ṣaḍdhātu*, *khams drug*) of form etc., and the fifth, the cognition that experiences it, to be the six constituents of consciousness. This establishes that there is a way in which objects such as form etc. appear to the eye consciousness and so forth through the power of the imprints of verbal expressions.

What is that? When blue appears to an eye consciousness seeing blue, there are three ways of appearing: blue appearing as blue; blue appearing as an external object; and that very blue appearing as the basis or locus of the convention “this is blue.” It is the last of these, because that appearance arises through the power of imprints deposited by familiarity with the conceptual mind’s having made, many times since beginningless time, conventions of entity and particulars, such as “this is blue,” “this is form,” and “this is the arising of form.” Therefore that appearance is called an appearance merely posited by names and linguistic signs, and that imprint is called an imprint of verbal expression.

How do we know that there is a way in which blue appears to the eye consciousness as blue that is the basis or locus of the convention?

The *Viniścayasaṃgrahaṇī* states:

“Here, one should understand that childish beings, for five reasons, grasp at the thing being expressed as having that nature just as it [is expressed by] its name and verbal expression. Thus, if asked, ‘What is the nature of this thing?’, they answer, ‘Its nature is form,’ and do not answer, ‘It is nominal form.’”

This first reason explains that when childish beings are asked, “What is the nature of the object expressed as ‘blue’?”, they grasp at blue as [existing] in the way the appearance of blue appears to that eye consciousness and answer, “Its nature is form”; they do not answer that what is merely designated by the name “form” is the nature of the object expressed as “form.”

Therefore, because the eye consciousness induces, through its own power, a conceptual mind that takes as its object of recollection that very appearance of blue as it appears to that eye consciousness and recollects it with the convention “this is blue,” [it is established] that there is a way in which blue appears to the eye consciousness as the basis of the convention “blue.” This object as it so appears to the eye consciousness is the conventionally existent completely imputed [nature].

Since the appearance to eye consciousness of blue as being established through its own characteristics as the basis for applying the conventions of entity and particulars is an appearance that arises through the power of the imprints of ignorance, what appears [to exist] in such a manner does not exist even conventionally. It is the completely imputed [nature] whose characteristics are entirely non-existent. Although both are alike in being merely designated by the conceptual mind, they differ as to whether or not they are merely posited by names and linguistic signs. For example, sound’s being empty of permanence is a completely imputed [nature], yet it does not become an object of cognition merely through the power of imprints through familiarity with the expression “sound is not permanent” and so forth, nor can it be posited merely by the convention saying “sound has no permanence.” So whatever is a completely imputed [nature] is not necessarily merely posited by names and linguistic signs.

Blue’s appearing as blue to the eye consciousness is the effect produced by imprints of similar type. An “imprint of similar type” is whatever potential of each earlier cognition to produce later cognitions similar to itself is imprinted onto and deposited in the foundation consciousness when [the earlier cognition] ceases. Asaṅga and his Brother hold that very imprint to be the causal condition among the four conditions. The object that appears in this way is called the other-powered, for the *Mahāyānasaṃgraha* states:

“Why is it called other-powered? Because it arises from its imprints that are its seeds, it is powered by other conditions. Once it has arisen, it cannot abide by itself for longer than a moment; therefore it is called powered by other.”

When one thus understands that, in the way [things] appear to a single eye consciousness, there are distinct traces placed by their respective imprints, one will understand well that the blue that appears as blue to the sense consciousness, the blue that appears as the basis of conventions, and the blue that appears as the basis of conventions established through its own characteristics are, respectively, the other-powered, the completely imputed that is the imputation of entities or particulars, and the completely imputed that is the object of negation.

Although the Sautrāntikas and Cittamātrins alike hold that a conceptual mind grasps at the appearance of form to a sense consciousness and creates the convention “this is form,” they disagree as to whether it is established or not through its own characteristics as it appears. When the *Bodhisattvabhūmi* refutes this superimposition, it refutes it by scripture as well; and since one cannot refute other schools by the scriptures of one’s own teacher, the [one] being refuted belongs to our own [Buddhist] schools. Since what it refutes is not the point on which the Proponents of Naturelessness and the Yogācāras differ, [those refuted] are of the Śrāvakayāna schools.

The scripture is as follows.

The *Bodhisattvabhūmi* states:

“This is, to begin with, in accordance with the reasoning established by valid means (*upapatti-sādhana-yukti*, *’thad pas sgrub pa’i rigs pa*); by it one should understand all dharmas to be of an inexpressible nature. One should also understand all dharmas to be of an inexpressible nature by trustworthy scripture, for in the *Bhavasaṃkrānti Sūtra* the Bhagavān too spoke this very meaning in verse:

‘By whatever names
Whatever dharmas are expressed,
They are not in them;
This is the dharmatā of dharmas.’”

Citing a Sūtra accepted by the Śrāvaka schools themselves, it proves that things are inexpressible. The way form and so forth are here posited as the referent (*zhen gzhi*) of the conceptual mind as not established through their own characteristics differs from the way the Sautrāntikas posit form and so forth as the determined object (*zhen yul*) of the conceptual mind as not established through their own characteristics. The former say that that object which appears to sense consciousnesses when form etc. appear as the referent of conventions such as “form,” is not established through its own characteristics. The latter say the self-isolate of the determined object of the conceptual mind is not established through its own characteristics.

**Second, the reasoning that refutes it.**

The *Mahāyānasaṃgraha* states:

“Because there is no cognition prior to the names,
Because there are many, and because they are not definite,
By contradictions of its nature, multiple natures
And natures being mixed [it is] established.”

It teaches three reasonings: since there is no cognition prior to the name, [there would be] the contradiction that [the object] has that [name] as its nature; since there are many names, [there would be] the contradiction that [it] has many natures; and since names are not definite, [there would be] the contradiction that [their] natures are mixed. The same occurs in the *Bodhisattvabhūmi* and the *Viniścayasaṃgrahaṇī*.

The first reasoning: Take the subject, a bulbous entity, it follows that, before the name “pot” was designated to it, the cognition thinking “pot” would arise with respect to it, because it is established as the referent of the convention “pot” without relying on linguistic signs, by virtue of the bulbous entity’s own objective mode of existence as a functional thing.

The proof implied [by this consequence]: Take the subject, a bulbous entity, it is not established by its own nature as the referent of the word “pot,” because, without the linguistic sign “pot” being applied to it, the cognition thinking “pot” does not arise with respect to it.

The second reasoning: just as many names such as Indra, Śakra and Purandara apply to a single person, it would follow that the object too abides as many, because it is established as the referent of the many names through its own characteristics.

The reason: Take the subject, that single person, they are not established through their own characteristics as the referent of many names such as Indra, Śakra and Purandara, because they are not many persons.

The third reasoning: just as the single name “Upagupta” applies in common to two persons, it would follow that the objects to which it applies are also one, because the two are established as the referent of a single name through their own characteristics.

The reason: Take the subject, the two, Upagupta the kṣatriya and Upagupta the brahmin: they are not established through their own characteristics as the referent of the single name “Upagupta,” because those two persons are not a single continuum.

According to the Sautrāntikas, if the actual object expressed by the word “pot” were established as specifically characterised, there would be the fault that the cognition of the name would arise without depending on the making of the connection [between word and object] through a linguistic sign; but they say that there is no such fault in [the bulbous thing’s] being established through its own characteristics in the way it appears to a sense consciousness as the referent of the name “pot.” The Vijñaptivādins press [the point] with these reasonings, [showing] that such a distinction cannot be drawn.

Although the Sautrāntikas do not hold that space which is the referent of the name “space” is a functional thing, they hold that the way space is established as the referent of the name “space” through its own objective mode of existence; and if it were not established in that way, they would not know how to posit space as existent.

If the presentation of the three characteristics is applied to uncompounded dharmas: space is the other-powered [nature] of space; space’s being established as the referent of the conceptual mind apprehending space through its own characteristics is the completely imputed [nature]; and it’s not being established through its own characteristics as the referent of the conceptual mind that apprehends it in that way is the thoroughly established [nature] of space. The same applies to the other uncompounded dharmas.

If one asks, “Since space is completely imputed, how do you explain its naturelessness of arising?”:

The *Saṃdhinirmocana* states:

“With naturelessness of characteristics in mind, I taught that all dharmas are unproduced, unceasing, quiescent from the beginning and naturally in a state of nirvāṇa. Why is that? Paramārthasamudgata, whatever does not exist through its own characteristics does not arise…”

and so forth. Since it thus teaches how uncompounded [dharmas] are naturelessness of arising, for something to be naturelessness in terms of arising it need not have arising.

**Third, how one enters cognisance-only by means of these reasonings.** If one asks how one enters cognisance-only by this way of refuting the extreme of superimposition, it is explained as follows:

The *Mahāyānasaṃgraha* states:

“Thus, a bodhisattva that has become one who strives to enter cognisance-only, correctly considers, with regard to the mental expression in which letters and objects appear, that the names that are letters, too, are no more than mere mental conceptuality. They correctly consider that the objects based on letters, too, are no more than mere mental expression. They correctly consider that names, too, amount to no more than mere designations of entities and particulars. Therefore, when they observe [them] as no more than mere mental expression, when they do not observe referents and their names along with designations of entities and particulars, and [do not observe] the entities and particulars as characteristics of the referent, then, through the four thorough searchings (*catur-vidha-paryeṣaṇā*, *yongs su tshol ba bzhi*) and the fourfold thorough knowledge in accordance with reality (*catur-vidha-yathābhūta-parijñāna*, *yang dag pa ji lta ba bzhin du yongs su shes pa bzhi*), they enter into those mental conceptualizations in which letters and objects appear as being cognisance-only.”

Thus it teaches that the Bodhisattva, in reliance on the four [thorough] searchings and knowledges, thinks “the object being determined by the mental conceptualisation to which—in dependence on expressing names, the expressed referent and the relation between the name and the referent—the entity and particulars of the expressed object appear as being established from the side of the object’s mode of being, does not exist as it appears. So there is also no unmistaken mode of apprehension that apprehends in that way.” In this way they enter cognisance-only free of the duality of subject and object.

The four searchings: the first searching looks for whether or not the name engages its referent through the power of [the object’s] mode of being. The second searching searches whether the referent is established as the basis of the name through the power of [its] mode of being. The third searching looks for whether that nature designated by the name of a given referent which designates an entity to it [by saying] “this is that dharma” is established through the power of the mode of being. The fourth searching searches for whether or not that nature designated by the name of a given referent which designates a particular [by saying] “that arises” is established through the power of the mode of being. The four realisations that these are not established in the way they were sought by the four searchings are the four thorough knowledges.

By the very refutation of this superimposition, subject and object being substantially different in dependence on external objects is effortlessly refuted. When reasoning refutes that form and so forth are established through their own characteristics as referents of conventions of entities and particulars, [their] existing in the way they appear to the sense consciousnesses to which they so appear is also refuted; this establishes that sense consciousnesses are mistaken with respect to their appearing objects. When that is established, [their] arising through the force of an external apprehended object is refuted, and blue and so forth existing as a separate substance from the sense consciousnesses to which they appear is also refuted.

If one does not understand how to refute the completely imputed existing through its own characteristics in this way as explicitly taught in the *Saṃdhinirmocana Sūtra*, one would have to explain wherever the Mother Sūtras speak of [things] being “non-existent” as only a refutation of the completely imputed in reference to the duality of subject and object. In that case, where it teaches the “non-existence of space,” one would have to explain its underlying intention as “the non-existence of an externally existent space.” This would be extremely unreasonable for the Yogācāra system.

Therefore one must know decisively what the completely imputed explained in the *Saṃdhinirmocana* is and is not, the reasoning that refutes it, and how, by those reasonings, subject and object being substantially different is refuted and one enters cognisance-only.

Although this [Cittamātra] system holds that the completely imputed is not established through its own characteristics, the Cittamātrins accept that even the completely imputed is established through its own characteristics according to [how it is presented as] the object of negation of the Mādhyamika Prāsaṅgikas; so although only the term “established through its own characteristics” is the same, the meaning differs greatly. Likewise, in the systems of those texts that explain that the appearing objects of conceptual minds and generally characterised [phenomena] are not established through their own characteristics, the meaning of being established through its own characteristics that is the Prāsaṅgikas’ object of negation [still] remains.

**Third, showing the purpose of knowing the entities of the three natures.** The *Saṃdhinirmocana* states:

“Sentient beings superimpose the completely imputed nature on the other-powered nature and the thoroughly established nature, and they subsequently designate the other-powered nature and the thoroughly established nature as the completely imputed nature. As they continue to designate [them] with such conventions in this way, their minds become saturated with such conventions, become enmeshed and predisposed to such conventions and start conceiving the other-powered and thoroughly established [natures] with the characteristics of the completely imputed nature.

“Conceiving of them in this way, this conception of the other-powered nature as a completely imputed nature acts as a cause and condition that gives rise to a later other-powered nature. As a result of this, they will be afflicted by the afflictions of defilements, karma and birth. [Consequently], sentient beings will transmigrate and wander for long periods among hell [beings], hungry ghosts, animals, devas, asuras or humans and not go beyond saṃsāra.

“Paramārthasamudgata, to those sentient beings who from the start have not generated roots of virtue, have not thoroughly purified their obscurations, have not thoroughly ripened their mental continuum, who have little devotion, and who have not accomplished the collections of merit and wisdom, I teach the dharma beginning with naturelessness of arising. Once they have heard this teaching, they understand that the conditioned phenomena that are dependent originations are impermanent, know them as unstable, unreliable and subject to change; they become disenchanted with all conditioned phenomena and weary of them. Having become disenchanted and weary, they turn away from wrongdoing; they do not commit any wrongdoing, and they rely on virtue. Through relying on virtue, they generate roots of virtue not yet generated, purify obscurations not yet purified, and ripen continuums not yet ripened. On that basis they come to have much devotion and to accomplish the collections of merit and wisdom.

“Although they thus generate roots of virtue and accomplish the collections of merit and wisdom, nevertheless, because they do not know the naturelessness of arising just as it is, as the two aspects of the naturelessness of characteristics and ultimate naturelessness, they do not become truly weary of all conditioned phenomena. They do not become truly free of attachment. They do not become truly liberated. They do not become completely free of the afflictions of the defilements, karma and birth.

“Therefore the Tathāgata teaches them the Dharma starting with naturelessness of characteristics and ultimate naturelessness, so that they may become truly weary of all conditioned phenomena, become free of attachment and become liberated, and truly pass beyond the afflictions of the defilements, karma and birth.

“Having heard such a teaching, they do not conceive of the other-powered nature with the characteristics of the completely imputed nature. Thus they feel drawn towards the naturelessness of arising as the naturelessness of characteristics and ultimate naturelessness. They discern and know it just as it is. It is like this: since their minds are no longer saturated with such conventions and no longer enmeshed or predisposed to such conventions, they attain the power of the knowledge of the other-powered characteristics in this life and put an end to them by completely cutting off their continuum. In this way, they become truly weary of conditioned phenomena, they become truly free of attachment, they attain true liberation, and they become completely free of the afflictions of the defilements, karma and birth.

“Paramārthasamudgata, sentient beings of the Śrāvaka-vehicle lineage too accomplish [the goal] and attain the unsurpassed bliss of nirvāṇa by this very path and this very practice; and sentient beings of the Pratyekabuddha-vehicle lineage and those of the Tathāgata lineage too accomplish [the goal] and attain the unsurpassed bliss of nirvāṇa by this very path and this very practice. Therefore this is the single path of purification of Śrāvakas, Pratyekabuddhas and Bodhisattvas, and there is only a single purification; there is no other. With that in mind I taught a single vehicle. It is not, however, that there are not, in the realm of sentient beings, various types of beings who by nature have weak faculties, middling faculties and sharp faculties.”

**Third, the explanation of the definitions of the two truths.** The ultimate truth is not posited merely by whether something exists ultimately or not, because the other-powered exists ultimately but is not an ultimate truth. The first [part] is established, because the *Saṃdhinirmocana Sūtra*, the texts of Asaṅga and his brother, the *Mahāyānasūtrālaṃkāra*, the *Madhyāntavibhāga* and others explain again and again that the other-powered exists ultimately. The second is established, because the *Saṃdhinirmocana* explains that, because the other-powered is not ultimate, it has ultimate naturelessness. Therefore the “ultimate” in “existing ultimately or not” refers to the object’s being established through its own characteristics rather than being posited through the power of the conceptual mind; and the “ultimate” in “ultimate truth” refers to uncontaminated meditative equipoise that is unmistaken with respect to the mode of existence.

That which is found by a reasoning consciousness which directly perceives true reality is the definition of ultimate truth. It possesses five distinctive features,

for the *Viniścayasaṃgrahaṇī* states:

“The ultimate possesses five characteristics: the characteristic of being inexpressible, the characteristic of being non-dual, the characteristic of completely transcending the domain of logicians, the characteristic of completely transcending difference and non-difference, and the characteristic of being of one taste everywhere.”

This and the thoroughly established are synonymous. Because the *Mahāyānasaṃgraha* teaches that the thoroughly established is to be understood by way of the four kinds of purified dharmas, the thoroughly established is naturally purified. Since that is explained as emptiness, emptiness, the ultimate and the thoroughly established are synonymous.

That which is found by a valid cognition that analyses conventions is the definition of conventional truth. When divided, it is twofold: the other-powered and the completely imputed.
